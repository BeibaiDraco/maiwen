# Turn an idea into a scientific question

Use the [field opportunity map](field-grounding.md) to generate questions across distinct scientific topics before refining estimators. For each promising idea, state the knowledge it could change and a plausible connection to the data; leave untested premises as questions to investigate. Develop the strongest ideas through literature and data checks, separating a known missing essential condition from support that has not yet been examined. Compare their scientific payoff and the genuinely different alternatives considered. A user direction prioritizes the search; a model, window or metric is chosen to answer the question.

Write a plain-language question with the system or population, the unknown quantity or relationship, the relevant conditions or timescale, and what an answer would establish. Name the specific knowledge at stake and explain what two plausible answers would change. Anchor a replication or challenge in a published claim, figure or estimate with a source locator; anchor a new phenomenon, descriptive or predictive question in a consequential unresolved observation, uncertainty or use. If the contribution is greater precision or a new method, explain which scientific uncertainty or practical decision it would resolve.

Distinguish the question from a hypothesis. The question names what is unknown; a hypothesis proposes an answer, explanation or relationship. Where useful, develop hypotheses from observations, theory, analogy or model structure, mark new extensions, and derive observable predictions under explicit measurement and auxiliary assumptions. Explore relevant alternatives, including combinations that can coexist. Predictions shared by different accounts motivate a more discriminating comparison or a narrower claim. Questions can guide hypotheses and hypotheses can reveal new questions; no fixed ordering or mechanism-hypothesis quota applies.

Describe the comparison that would answer it and the strongest reason it might fail or be uninteresting. Compare with the closest work, including later answers to an older paper's limitation. Say what remains to learn, why it matters and what finding in the literature would make this question unnecessary.

Separate the scientific object from its operational proxy. Write candidate definitions, the population and independent unit, and what would make a scientifically meaningful difference. Select definitions for their relation to the object, then inspect support. If reasonable definitions change coverage, report the sensitivity; do not select one because its effect is favorable or its count is larger.

Use descriptive and predictive aims on their own merits. Observational evidence can constrain explanations without establishing a mechanism; causal claims need an argued identification strategy. Replication needs a named claim, a reason its uncertainty matters, and a comparison that genuinely addresses it. A familiar result is not automatically trivial.

When a limitation narrows the question, save a short before/after with the evidence, remaining scientific payoff and changed nearest-work comparison. If a process or population question becomes a description of the available records, explain what useful uncertainty that description can resolve and what would still be required for the original claim. Judge bounds and robustness checks by the uncertainty they resolve. If the remaining payoff is unclear or insufficient, compare a scientifically different opportunity within the remaining budget, or park the direction with the smallest useful next check. Continue developing the question when its remaining contribution justifies the work.
