# Turn an idea into a scientific question

Use the [field opportunity map](field-grounding.md) to generate questions across distinct scientific topics before refining estimators. For each promising idea, state the knowledge it could change and a plausible connection to the data; leave untested premises as questions to investigate. Develop the strongest ideas through literature and data checks, separating a known missing essential condition from support that has not yet been examined. Compare their scientific payoff and the genuinely different alternatives considered. A user direction prioritizes the search; a model, window or metric is chosen to answer the question.

Write a plain-language question with an object or population, an unknown quantity or relationship, a context or timescale, and the intended claim. Name the specific knowledge at stake and explain what two plausible answers would change. Anchor a replication or challenge in a published claim, figure or estimate with a source locator; anchor a new phenomenon, descriptive or predictive question in a consequential unresolved observation, uncertainty or use. “More precise” or “a new method” alone is not a value argument.

Describe the comparison that would answer it and the strongest reason it might fail or be uninteresting. Compare with the closest work, including later answers to an older paper's limitation. Say what remains to learn, why it matters and what finding in the literature would make this question unnecessary.

Separate the scientific object from its operational proxy. Write candidate definitions, the population and independent unit, and what would make a scientifically meaningful difference. Select definitions for their relation to the object, then inspect support. If reasonable definitions change coverage, report the sensitivity; do not select one because its effect is favorable or its count is larger.

Use descriptive and predictive aims on their own merits. Observational evidence can constrain explanations without establishing a mechanism; causal claims need an argued identification strategy. Replication needs a named claim, a reason its uncertainty matters, and a comparison that genuinely addresses it. A familiar result is not automatically trivial.

When a limitation narrows the question, save a short before/after with the evidence, remaining scientific payoff and changed nearest-work comparison. If only a bound or robustness check remains, identify which uncertainty it would resolve and why that uncertainty matters. If that argument fails, return to unused data opportunities or a different paper's reasoning within the remaining budget, or explain why no worthwhile route remains. Do not preserve a low-value question merely because its design is already detailed.
