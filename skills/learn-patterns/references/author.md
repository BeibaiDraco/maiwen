# Author paper cases and question patterns

Read the collection objective, supplied sources or existing bank, source-access restrictions and budget. Own the assigned library files, leaving `review.md` to the independent reviewer. Write English. In a new collection, use papers whose scientific reasoning is instructive, including different designs or systems where useful; distinguish influential work from merely highly cited work. With local PDFs, inspect relevant methods, figures and equations. Without them, find accessible primary versions through host tools. Respect private-document permissions and preserve originals.

## Reconstruct cases before generalizing

Use [paper notes](paper-notes.md) for consequential studies and write `cases/<paper>.md`. Reconstruct the visible scientific argument: prior knowledge and unresolved tension; the relevant data and design conditions; the decisive measurement, comparison or redefinition; why it addresses the tension; actual findings, surviving alternatives and scientific consequences. Explain separately how the worthwhile unknown was formulated and, where supported, how a proposed explanation or relationship was constructed. For the latter, trace the motivating observation or theory, assumptions, predicted observations and competing accounts. Preserve their interaction without inventing a historical sequence or forcing both moves into every paper. Add necessary conditions and nontransferable details. Source the reconstruction with page, figure, equation or methods locators and label interpretation as such. Do not invent an author's historical thinking.

Record sources selected and why in `source-index.md`, including stable citation, decisive locator, reading depth, version/date and access limits. Distinguish optional local source locations from files carried with the bank; a moved bank must explain how to locate omitted sources rather than promise broken relative PDF paths. Reviews help find cases but do not substitute for primary evidence behind a claimed pattern. Follow contrasting or later studies when they change the inference. A source's sample size is not a universal rule; capture the inferential condition it helped meet.

## Synthesize a usable guide

Write `question-patterns.md` as an intelligible guide to forming questions. For each useful move, explain:

- The observation, tension or knowledge gap that calls for it, and how it transforms that starting point into a worthwhile question.
- Where supported, the separate hypothesis-forming move: how observations, a theory, an analogy or a model suggest a proposed answer, what assumptions link it to observable predictions, and what would distinguish relevant alternatives. Label a proposed extension separately from the source argument.
- The comparison or measurement that can distinguish something previously conflated, and why the resulting answer matters.
- The supporting cases and what their differences teach; a claim of cross-study support needs multiple independent cases.
- Essential observations, variation, design and inferential assumptions; optional strengths and incidental source details.
- A concrete failed transfer or counterexample, with the missing condition explained.
- A concrete adaptation showing the starting situation, the question and any hypothesis it motivates, the decisive comparison, and what different answers would change. Name what must be supplied by the new project; an attractive conditional statement alone is not a usable procedure.

Give each pattern a descriptive name that helps a researcher recognize when to use it. Distinguish one-case inspiration from a defensible cross-case generalization. Consolidate overlapping moves only when their inferential logic matches. Where a broad family contains different comparisons, make its branches selectable: explain the starting scientific situation, the operation to perform and what choice it requires. Explain which normalization, baseline or predicted observation each comparison requires, and why the alternatives differ. Show the selected operation in the worked adaptation. Index positive routes such as explaining a regularity, linking levels of observation, deriving a theory's new prediction or explaining conditional advantages, alongside routes that resolve ambiguity. Let the scientific content determine the number of patterns. See the [fictional worked example](worked-example.md) for depth and structure; it supplies no scientific evidence for a real bank.

Keep field-specific examples and limitations while making the reasoning understandable outside the source dataset. Do not insert assumed facts about a new target dataset into the reusable claim. A library can teach methods without a target dataset; project-specific selection belongs in the project's knowledge notes.

## Return, revise and preserve

For reuse, identify the existing reviewed revision and inspect relevant source evidence, changed conditions and new contradictions. Preserve unchanged useful material; make a focused update rather than recreating the bank. Keep an earlier reviewed copy before changing substantive claims, and link its successor from the index. Return paths, evidence depth, meaningful changes and unresolved limits to the coordinator. Do not write your own independent approval or spawn agents.

When review arrives, verify its objections and suggested replacements against sources, revise the affected cases/patterns and report exact changes. Identify newly added or strengthened scientific claims and the passage or argument supporting them, including claims introduced while correcting factual details. After narrowing an inference, explain what worthwhile question or hypothesis-forming move remains; if that contribution disappears, revise the move or leave it provisional. A reviewer proposal is not automatically correct. Stay within the shared repair allowance; leave unsupported generalization provisional rather than filling it with an unsupported conclusion.
